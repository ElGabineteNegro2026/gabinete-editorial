**I. CHRONOLOGY**

**1833.** Edgar Allan Poe’s “MS. Found in a Bottle” wins the prize of the Baltimore *Saturday Visiter*.

**1841.** Poe publishes “A Descent into the Maelström” in *Graham’s Magazine*.

**1872.** The brigantine *Mary Celeste* is found drifting and abandoned between the Azores and Portugal.

**1880.** Arthur Conan Doyle, a medical student, sails to the Arctic as surgeon of the whaler *Hope*.

**1883.** Doyle publishes “The Captain of the Pole-Star” in *Temple Bar*.

**1884.** “J. Habakuk Jephson’s Statement” appears anonymously in the *Cornhill Magazine*.

**1886.** F. Marion Crawford publishes “The Upper Berth”.

**1892.** Rudyard Kipling publishes “A Matter of Fact”, collected in *Many Inventions* (1893).

**1896.** H. G. Wells publishes “In the Abyss” in *Pearson’s Magazine* and “The Sea Raiders” in *The Weekly Sun Literary Supplement*.

**1898.** Morgan Robertson publishes *Futility*, the novel about the unsinkable *Titan*.

**1899.** Robert W. Chambers publishes the first version of “The Harbor-Master”.

**1904.** Chambers revises the story as the opening chapters of *In Search of the Unknown*.

**1907.** William Hope Hodgson publishes “The Voice in the Night” in the *Blue Book Magazine*.

**1912.** The *Titanic* sinks after striking an iceberg in the North Atlantic.

**1913.** Irvin S. Cobb publishes “Fishhead” in *The Cavalier*.

**1914.** Robertson collects “From the Darkness and the Depths” in *The Grain Ship*.

**1917.** H. P. Lovecraft writes “Dagon”, published in *The Vagrant* (1919) and *Weird Tales* (1923).

**1925.** “The Temple”, written in 1920, appears in *Weird Tales*.

**1927.** Lovecraft completes *Supernatural Horror in Literature*, in which he praises Poe, Chambers, Hodgson and Cobb.

**1928.** “The Call of Cthulhu” appears in *Weird Tales*. John Buchan publishes “Skule Skerry” in *The Runagates Club*.

**1930.** William Beebe and Otis Barton make their first descents in the bathysphere off Bermuda: the first human beings to see the deep sea alive.

***

**II. THE SEA SERPENT AND THE KRAKEN**

The monsters of these stories had long ancestries. Norwegian sailors told of the kraken, a creature so vast that it could be mistaken for an island, and in 1752 Erik Pontoppidan, Bishop of Bergen, described it in his *Natural History of Norway* alongside the sea serpent, as soberly as he described the herring. Through the nineteenth century, sightings of sea serpents were reported in the newspapers and debated in learned societies; the most famous, from HMS *Daedalus* in 1848, was signed by the ship’s captain and sent to the Admiralty.

The giant squid, which gives so much to Wells and Lovecraft, was long thought to be one of these legends. It was not until the 1870s, when several carcasses were washed ashore in Newfoundland and studied by naturalists, that science accepted it as real. No living giant squid was photographed in its natural habitat until 2004. The writers of this book were working in the gap between rumour and proof, and they knew it.

***

**III. THE DEEP SEA BEFORE THE BATHYSPHERE**

Until the middle of the nineteenth century, many naturalists believed that nothing could live in the deep ocean: the cold, the darkness and the pressure seemed to make life impossible. The dredgings of the 1860s and above all the voyage of HMS *Challenger* (1872–1876), which sounded every ocean and brought up thousands of unknown species from depths of several miles, proved the contrary. The deep was full of life, and much of it was strange beyond anything the naturalists had imagined: blind fish, luminous creatures, animals made almost entirely of jelly.

Wells’s “In the Abyss”, with its glowing creatures and its city on the ocean floor, is a direct response to that discovery. So, in a more distant way, are the drowned cities of Lovecraft. When William Beebe finally went down in his bathysphere in 1930 and described the lights he saw through its window, his reports read, to many of his readers, like a page from these stories.

***

**IV. THE DROWNED CITY**

The legend of a city or a continent lost beneath the sea goes back to Plato, whose dialogues *Timaeus* and *Critias* describe Atlantis, the great island power that sank “in a single day and night of misfortune”. The nineteenth century revived it: Ignatius Donnelly’s *Atlantis: The Antediluvian World* (1882) was a best-seller, and the theosophists added Lemuria and Mu to the list of vanished continents. Lovecraft knew this literature well and borrowed from it freely, but he turned it around. His sunken cities, Y’ha-nthlei and R’lyeh, were never built by men; they belong to beings who were here first, and who are waiting.

***

**V. LOVECRAFT AND HIS PREDECESSORS**

Lovecraft did not invent the fish-men, the hidden races of the sea or the sense of a cosmic indifference behind the waves; he inherited them. In *Supernatural Horror in Literature*, the long essay he completed in 1927, he gave pride of place to Poe, praised Chambers’s early weird tales, ranked Hodgson among the masters of the form and described Cobb’s “Fishhead” as “banefully effective in its portrayal of unnatural affinities between a hybrid idiot and the strange fish of an isolated lake”. “The Harbor-Master” and “Fishhead” in particular are often read as forerunners of *The Shadow over Innsmouth* (1936). Reading them alongside Lovecraft’s own stories shows both how much he took and how much he added.

**VI. ABOUT THIS EDITION**

The texts follow the first editions in book form or the earliest reliable printings: Poe’s *Tales*; Doyle’s *The Captain of the Polestar and Other Tales* (1890); Crawford’s *The Upper Berth* (1894); Kipling’s *Many Inventions* (1893); Wells’s *The Plattner Story and Others* (1897); Chambers’s *In Search of the Unknown* (1904); the *Blue Book Magazine* for Hodgson; *The Cavalier* for Cobb; *The Grain Ship* (1914) for Robertson; *Weird Tales* for the three stories by Lovecraft; and *The Runagates Club* (1928) for Buchan. Original spelling, British or American, has been kept, with only obvious misprints corrected. The introductory notes and these notes on the stories are by the editor.

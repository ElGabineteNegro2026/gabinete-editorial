The sea was the first great unknown, and for most of human history it was the last. Mountains could be climbed, deserts crossed and forests cleared, but the sea kept its depths to itself. A sailor could see a few fathoms down on a calm day; below that there was only darkness, and in the darkness, anything at all. The old maps filled the empty water with serpents and whales the size of islands, and the sailors who drew them were not entirely joking. Even now, when we have photographed the far side of the moon, we have seen less of the floor of our own oceans than of the surface of Mars.

This book gathers fifteen stories about that darkness, written between 1833 and 1928, the century in which the sea stopped being a matter of legend and became a matter of science, without becoming any less frightening. They are arranged in the order in which they appeared, so that the reader can follow the way the fear changes: from the whirlpool and the ghost ship to the deep-sea creature, from the creature to the hidden race, and from the hidden race to the gods who are only sleeping.

It begins with Edgar Allan Poe. “MS. Found in a Bottle” and “A Descent into the Maelström” are not monster stories; their horror is the sea itself, the current that drags a ship towards the pole, the vortex that swallows fishing boats whole. Poe’s narrators are carried to the edge of the world and look over it. What they see is grand and terrible and impossible to describe, and it is no accident that both stories end with a man who has come back changed, or has not come back at all.

The Victorian writers who followed brought the sea closer to home. Arthur Conan Doyle had sailed to the Arctic as a young ship’s surgeon, and “The Captain of the Pole-Star” is full of the real ice and the real superstitions of a whaling crew. In “J. Habakuk Jephson’s Statement” he took the most famous mystery of the nineteenth-century sea, the abandoned *Mary Celeste*, and invented an answer so convincing that it was taken for fact. F. Marion Crawford’s “The Upper Berth” brings the haunting indoors, into a first-class cabin on a transatlantic liner, and shows that the sea does not need to be seen to be felt: it is enough to find the porthole open and the berth wet.

With H. G. Wells, at the end of the century, the sea becomes a laboratory. “In the Abyss” sends a man down in a steel sphere, and “The Sea Raiders” describes a new species of cephalopod in the language of a scientific journal. Wells is careful, plausible and modern, and that is exactly what makes these stories unsettling: they ask us to imagine that the deep is not empty but inhabited, and that its inhabitants have simply not noticed us yet. Robert W. Chambers took the next step in “The Harbor-Master”, where the creatures of the shoals look disturbingly like men, and William Hope Hodgson, who had spent years at sea before he began to write, took it further still in “The Voice in the Night”, where the line between the man and the thing that grows on him disappears altogether.

In the years before and after the First World War the sea story turned darker and stranger. Irvin S. Cobb’s “Fishhead” moves the horror from the ocean to a swamp lake in Tennessee, and makes it a matter of blood and kinship; Morgan Robertson, a sailor who had predicted the loss of the *Titanic* fourteen years before it happened, offers a painter’s account of what lives in the deepest water. And then comes H. P. Lovecraft, the writer who gathered all these threads together. “Dagon”, “The Temple” and “The Call of Cthulhu” take the hidden races and drowned cities of his predecessors and turn them into a mythology: the sea is the home of beings older than mankind, indifferent to it and bound to return. Lovecraft read most of the authors in this book, admired several of them in his essay *Supernatural Horror in Literature*, and learned from all of them. The book closes with John Buchan’s “Skule Skerry”, in which a level-headed ornithologist alone on a rock in the northern ocean discovers that the old sailors’ terrors were not superstition but a kind of knowledge.

Read in sequence, the stories trace a curious paradox. The more the sea was charted, sounded and explained, the more frightening it became in fiction. The sailors of Poe’s time feared storms and whirlpools; the readers of Lovecraft’s feared what science itself suggested, that the world is very old, that we are very recent and that the deep has never belonged to us. Each story in this volume is introduced by a short note on its author and its first publication, and the notes at the end of the book provide a chronology and some further background. The texts follow the first editions or the earliest reliable editions, with spelling and punctuation left as their authors wrote them.

One last suggestion. These stories were written to be read by lamplight, preferably with the sound of water somewhere not too far away. They work best that way still.
